Read this before using the 720p columns

You may not be able to order 720p at all

Google's API does not let the analyzer request a resolution ○. The camera trait advertises a 480p–1080p range, but quality is set per camera in the Google Home app (Low / Medium / High / Max) — the same setting that governs what store staff see when they open the app. Nobody has confirmed which app setting yields exactly 720p, or what bitrate it produces.

Buy against the 1080p columns. Treat 720p as an optimisation to test once the system is running, not a specification to order against.

A CPU that looks modern but is not, for this job

Intel Core Ultra mini PCs (e.g. the GMKtec K9, Core Ultra 5 125H, ~$361) sit in the slowest row: consumer Intel has shipped no AVX-512 since Alder Lake. They do carry an NPU that OpenVINO can target, which might move them up several tiers — but there is no NPU in this environment and no number will be put on it ○. One machine and one afternoon settles it. Until then, do not pay the premium expecting speed.

What happens at the margin

If a store adds a camera, nothing breaks. Two 8-core boxes carry 21 with five cameras spare; you can reach 26 before either is full. Past that, the failure mode is the thing everyone needs to understand:

The failure is silent, and that is the danger

An overloaded box does not crash. It quietly counts fewer people.

Measured on a deliberately overloaded machine ●: as cameras were added past capacity, the detection rate fell from 6/s to 5.4, then 4.4, and the decoder began falling behind the live stream (30 fps → 27.7 → 17.0). Nothing errored. Nothing alarmed. The analyzer simply looked at each doorway less often — and a person walking briskly through a door can cross the counting line between two samples and never be counted at all.

On the dashboard that appears as a gentle decline in footfall, indistinguishable from a quiet fortnight. Before this goes live the analyzer should report its achieved detection rate per camera, so an overloaded box announces itself instead of being found in a quarterly review. That work is not done.

If one box dies, the survivor cannot take all 21 cameras at detect 6/s — it holds 13. It can take all 21 at about detect 2/s ◐, which keeps every store counting at reduced confidence rather than leaving half of them blind. Write that configuration down now and test it once; it is not something to work out during the outage.

The question that was asked: is entrance-only counting much cheaper?

On fast hardware, barely. On slow hardware, enormously.

Counting people at a door and watching a whole floor use the same person detection at the same rate. The difference is only what happens to the boxes afterwards, and that costs about 2 CPU-ms per second ● — nothing. The saving that does exist comes from running floor cameras at a slower detection rate, since floor occupancy accumulates person-seconds and tolerates a one-second sample where a doorway does not.

But on the fast path 62% of a camera's cost is fixed: receiving the stream, decoding it, and running the loop — all paid the moment the camera is opened at all ◐. Dropping a camera from 6 detections a second to 1 saves only 32%. Across the realistic LuxeLink mix — 18 entrance cameras plus 3 watching the floor — the whole estate needs 8.5 cores instead of 8.9. A 5% saving. It does not change what you buy.

The inversion is worth knowing. On an AVX2-only machine the fixed share is only 30%, so the same change saves 58% there. The slower the hardware, the more the detection dials matter; the faster it is, the more you are simply paying to hold streams open. The one saving that is always real is not opening cameras you are not using — and the analyzer already skips any camera without Analytics ticked.

05

Before you spend money

Three commands, in order. The first needs no camera, no network and no account.

# 1. On the machine you are considering — does it carry what this table says?
python3 backend/vision_edge_analyzer.py --benchmark --benchmark-res 1080p

# 2. On one real store camera — what does Google actually send, and what does it cost?
python3 scratchpad/perf/measure_nest_stream.py --api https://… --seconds 120 --detect

# 3. Rebuild this whole table from what those two produced.
python3 scratchpad/perf/capacity_model.py --cores 8 --measure
python3 scratchpad/perf/buying_table.py

Command 2 is the important one and it has not been run. There is no Nest camera in the development environment, so every figure in this document that touches a real camera — the frame rate, the bitrate, the bandwidth — rests on synthetic video encoded at 2 Mbps ○. If Google turns out to send 15 fps, every machine above holds about 29% more cameras than stated. If it sends 4 Mbps, the bandwidth doubles.

One more check that has nothing to do with hardware

Google limits how many commands an account may send per minute, and holding 21 streams open means renewing each one every few minutes. Documented at 5 per minute per camera; reported — but not confirmed here ○ — as 10 per minute across a whole account. At 21 cameras we would sit around 6.3. Splitting the two metros across two Google accounts doubles that ceiling for free, and is worth doing whichever hardware is chosen.
